From AI Competencies to Curriculum

Complete facilitator and assessment guide

Status: Public portfolio adaptation of an original professional learning experience designed and developed by Heather Mullins, Ed.D. Materials and scenarios have been modified to protect institutionally restricted content. The public adaptation has not been implemented or formally evaluated.
Audience: Engineering and Computing Sciences faculty preparing to teach in the AI and Data Analytics ATC; faculty scenarios and instructional examples are adapted for portfolio demonstration.
Worked example: CAP3744 Data Analytics and Visualization.
Format: Self-paced design lab followed by a 60-minute facilitated design studio.

Purpose and performance outcomes

Faculty translate AI workforce competencies into assessable learning outcomes and authentic student evidence. By the end of the blended experience, participants will be able to (1) critique a weak learning outcome, (2) revise it using Actor, Behavior, Context, Degree, and Employability, (3) independently align one selected competency to a student task, observable evidence, performance standard, and evaluation method, (4) collaboratively extend that alignment to the remaining two competency dimensions, and (5) justify a program-level gap, duplication, or progression decision.

Before the workshop

Participants complete the self-paced module and bring their exported design brief. The brief includes their dashboard rationale, assessment-option critique, flawed-outcome diagnosis, ABCDE outcome, assessment brief, one independently developed evidence alignment, and program-transfer recommendation. Facilitator reviews submissions for patterns, not presumed proficiency. Prepare a shared three-row alignment worksheet and rubric; do not require participants to retype completed work.

Scenario and assessment calibration

Fictional equipment failure records show A: 48 failures/40,000 hours = 1.2 per 1,000 hours; B: 32/10,000 = 3.2; C: 21/10,000 = 2.1. Missing identifiers and incomplete records limit inference. The strongest proposed student assessment requires independent normalization, audit of missingness, comparison of defensible responses, and reproducible documentation. A platform screenshot demonstrates interface operation but not validation; a persuasive memo based on a supplied AI conclusion demonstrates communication but not independent analytical judgment. Do not reward one predetermined AI decision: evidence-supported use, delay, redesign, and refusal can all be defensible.

Outcome critique and exemplar

Weak: “Students will understand AI dashboards and use AI effectively to improve maintenance decisions.” The verbs are unobservable; context and quality standards are unspecified; AI use is presupposed. A stronger illustrative outcome: “Given maintenance records and an AI-generated reliability recommendation, students will independently normalize failure rates, document data-quality limitations, and justify a maintenance decision using reproducible calculations and explicitly stated evidence and risk criteria to support responsible engineering practice.” This is a model, not wording participants must copy.

60-minute facilitated design studio

Time Activity Evidence produced
0–8 min Revisit dashboard findings and individual ABCDE outcomes; surface differing defensible decisions Explicit design intent
8–20 min Exchange independently completed alignment and conduct evidence-based peer review Written comments tied to task, evidence, standard, and method
20–38 min In pairs, develop the two remaining competency alignments Three-row matrix: one independently authored, two collaboratively developed
38–48 min Audit the full matrix for overlap, gaps, evidence quality, and technically justified refusal/redesign Revised assessment criteria
48–56 min Evaluate the fictional ATC handoff: gap, duplication, or purposeful progression Program-level recommendation
56–60 min Document one substantive before/after revision and an implementation question Revised artifact and next step

Peer-review protocol

Reviewers cite exact language from the faculty artifact, then ask: Is the student task authentic? Can evidence be verified independently of an AI output? Does the performance standard define quality, not just completion? Does the method measure the claimed competency? Can different technically defensible conclusions meet the standard? Does the design contribute meaningfully to program progression? The author revises at least one weakness and records the reason.

Three-row alignment reference (annotated exemplar)

Competency Student task Observable evidence Proficient standard Evaluation method
Technical capability Normalize and audit failure rates Reproducible calculation and data-quality log Correct denominators, traceable transformations, material missingness identified Calculation check and artifact audit
Critical judgment Compare competing interpretations Annotated visualization and uncertainty rationale Exposure-adjusted interpretation and at least two decision-relevant limitations Analytic rubric and rationale review
Student agency Defend use, delay, redesign, or refusal Decision memo addressing alternatives and consequences Evidence-supported choice, safeguards, and stakeholder implications; no mandated conclusion Decision rubric and questioning

Flawed exemplar: “Generate an AI memo and submit a screenshot; full credit for on-time submission.” It lacks independent evidence, performance standards, and a basis for judging agency.

Behaviorally anchored rubric

Score each criterion 0 (not yet), 1 (developing), or 2 (proficient). Scores are formative and have not been validated.

Criterion 0 — Not yet 1 — Developing 2 — Proficient
Outcome quality Vague or unobservable Some ABCDE elements Observable behavior, context, degree, and professional purpose
Technical evidence Tool output only Verification mentioned Traceable, independently verifiable work and explicit standards
Judgment and agency Prescribed conclusion Alternatives possible but not evaluated Evidence-based alternatives and consequences evaluated
Evidence alignment Activity without assessable evidence Deliverables present but weak standards/methods Task, evidence, standard, and method align for each competency
Program transfer No relationship Relationship named without justification Defensible gap/duplication/progression and concrete handoff action

Evaluation plan and limits

Compare draft and revised faculty artifacts using the same rubric; use two reviewers when feasible. Collect participant feedback about clarity, workload, and usability, and conduct follow-up only if the design is implemented. Do not equate completed fields with demonstrated proficiency. No pilot outcomes, validated learning gains, or formal WCAG conformance are claimed for this public portfolio adaptation. Manual assistive-technology testing remains outstanding.
